### **Risk Domain & Regulatory Conflict Matrix**

| Risk Domain | Vulnerability & Technical Baselines (Source-Grounded) | Relevant Precedent/Law | Impact Level |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Legal Information Accuracy** | According to ***Profiling Legal Hallucinations in Large Language Models (arXiv:2401.01301v2)***, users over-relying on LLM outputs risk accepting false **"legal fictions"**. In reference-based federal case tasks, GPT-4 hallucinates facts or case citations at least **58%** of the time, while PaLM 2 hallucinates **72%** and Llama 2 hallucinates up to **88%** of the time. Additionally, LLMs systematically struggle to predict their own hallucinations and overestimate their confidence prior to post-hoc calibration, causing them to generate inaccurate information with extreme conviction. | Judicial sanctions and professional negligence under civil law, illustrated by the public apology issued to a US federal judge by law firm Sullivan & Cromwell after a court filing contained errors and incorrect case citations attributed to AI hallucinations, as detailed in ***Library (Caversham House)***. | **Severe** — Results in immediate judicial sanctions, systemic reputational damage to professional services firms, and a legal monoculture that flattens geographic and jurisdictional nuances. |
| **Training Data Privacy** | As demonstrated in ***A Comprehensive Study on Training Data PII Extraction Attacks in LLMs - arXiv***, system administrators severely underestimate privacy leakage risks based only on single-query assessments, whereas multi-query attacks employing advanced adversarial strategies increase PII extraction rates up to **fivefold** on pretrained models. Furthermore, fine-tuned models exhibit significantly elevated privacy risks, with simple subject-name template prompts achieving over **50%** extraction accuracy with modest query budgets (e.g., 256 queries). | EU General Data Protection Regulation (**GDPR**; Regulation (EU) 2016/679), under which companies face enforcement actions for failing to protect sensitive user information—for example, Spotify was fined approximately **€4–€5 million** for non-compliance with GDPR rules, as noted in ***AI Ethics and Future Impact (EquityMatch)***. | **Severe** — Elevates regulatory investigation risks, forces the deployment of costly defensive scrubbing pipelines that can still be bypassed under repeated queries, and risks heavy GDPR or AI Act regulatory fines. |
| **Automated Customer Channels** | According to ***AIR-2026-003: Moffatt v Air Canada (AI Agent Incident Register - CompanyScope)***, organizations deploying unmonitored automated chatbots risk binding themselves to **"invented"** policies that directly contradict their official systems of record. Consumers lack the technical capacity to verify dynamic AI advice and may reasonably rely on chatbot statements to their detriment, creating a direct cause of action against the deployer. | Common law tort of **negligent misrepresentation** (applying elements of the Supreme Court of Canada's *Queen v Cognos* decision) as established in *Moffatt v Air Canada* (2024 BCCRT 149), where the tribunal held that a company is legally bound by its chatbot's inaccurate representations. Equivalent UK principles of negligent misstatement under *Hedley Byrne* and the **Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (UCPD)** in the EU also apply. | **Severe** — Attaches primary civil liability directly to the deploying enterprise, negates standard contractual/tariff defenses when unproduced, forces immediate channel deactivation, and drives regulatory scrutiny under digital fairness checks. |
| **Consumer Transparency & Steering** | As detailed in ***Who Decides Whether AI Is Telling the Truth? (American Impact)***, AI developers may engage in **"undisclosed output steering,"** where models are secretly directed away from users' expected objectives (such as accurate or neutral information) toward developer priorities like safety, ideological filters, or commercial promotion. This hidden manipulation materially affects consumers, who accept AI outputs without fact-checking over **90%** of the time. | **Section 5 of the FTC Act** (**15 U.S.C. § 45**), which prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices, and the FTC's proposed policy statement (July 1, 2026) targeting the undisclosed suppression of AI accuracy. | **High to Severe** — Triggers federal deceptive marketing enforcement, eliminates standard safe harbors, and requires persistent, conspicuous disclosures that change user expectations. |
| **Technical Limitations vs. Deceptive Claims** | According to ***Who Decides Whether AI Is Telling the Truth? (American Impact)***, AI systems frequently generate factual inconsistencies due to incomplete, outdated, or contradictory training data. The Vectara Hallucination Leaderboard (May 11, 2026 results) shows a baseline factual inconsistency rate of **3%–5%** for stronger models, and over **20%** for lower-performing models, making absolute neutrality claims deceptive. | **Section 5 of the FTC Act** (**15 U.S.C. § 45**), where the Commission notes that while hallucinations from technical limits do not by themselves violate the law, making misleading marketing claims about their accuracy remains deceptive. | **Severe** — Exposes developers to enforcement actions under the FTC Act for deceptive marketing of unreliable models as objective or 100% accurate. |
| **Algorithmic Discrimination** | As analyzed in ***Who Decides Whether AI Is Telling the Truth? (American Impact)***, companies deploying high-risk systems are caught in a major regulatory conflict between state-mandated algorithmic discrimination protections—which may require developers to alter outputs—and federal deception rules that target undisclosed modifications. | Colorado state laws **SB 24-205** and **SB 26-189** (requiring risk-management programs and impact assessments to prevent algorithmic discrimination) vs. the Federal Trade Commission's (FTC) legal theory of implied federal preemption under Section 5. | **Severe** — Exposes organizations to legal uncertainty, conflicting regulatory mandates, state-level litigation for discriminatory outcomes, and potential federal preemption battles. |
| **Extraterritorial Jurisdiction** | According to ***EU AI Act 2026 Compliance Guide for US Companies (Tredence)***, US SaaS and AI providers frequently fail to realize that having "no EU office" does not shield them from jurisdiction if their systems or model outputs touch EU-based users. Operating high-risk use cases (e.g., HR resume screening or credit scoring) without a documented risk-management system, conformity assessment, and post-deployment monitoring is a severe compliance failure. | The European Union **Artificial Intelligence Act (AIA) 2026**, which asserts extraterritorial reach under Article 2 and imposes strict binding rules on high-risk applications, as detailed in ***AI Risk Management Frameworks (AWS)***. | **Critical** — Subjects non-compliant US companies to market access bans, product suspensions, and catastrophic administrative fines of up to **€35 million or 7% of global annual turnover**. |
